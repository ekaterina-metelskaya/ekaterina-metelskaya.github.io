## Context {#context}

I worked on a new internal product for managing documentation. At first, the task seemed straightforward: bring information scattered across different sources into one place to make it easier to work with.

But there was a problem. The expected economic benefit of the initial solution wasn't enough to justify approving its development.

Conversations with stakeholders revealed that centralized storage alone wouldn't solve the problem. Users needed not only to find information but also to understand whether it was up to date and complete. This opened up opportunities to make the product more valuable to the business.

## Where I started {#start}

I started by interviewing specialists who worked with documentation. At the same time, I reviewed the materials and documents they provided to understand users' tasks rather than design an interface based solely on the initial requests.

During the research, I looked beyond the proposed features and explored ways to make the product more useful to the business.

Based on what I learned, I developed user flows and requirements so the development team would have a clear understanding of how the system should work.

## More than a place to store information {#solution-1 label="Solution 01" toc="Business value" sub="01"}

The original business request was to create a system for storing information centrally. But the expected economic benefit was too small for the project to be approved.

I began looking for other ways the product could help. Interviews showed that users needed not just access to information but also a way to understand its current state. That led to the idea of adding analytics capabilities to the system.

[[solution-1-fig]]

An idea alone wasn't enough, though. We needed evidence that the additional capabilities would deliver value to the business.

I suggested that stakeholders test the value of the analytics using a practical example. The results strengthened the business case, and the project was approved for development.

> **What I learned**
>
> Sometimes a designer's job is not only to find a good solution for users but also to understand the value it could bring to the business. This matters especially when the original request isn't enough to get a product off the ground.

## Analytics where users need them {#solution-2 label="Solution 02" toc="Analytics in context" sub="02"}

Once we had agreed on the direction for the product, the next question was how to make analytics part of users' everyday work.

I didn't treat analytics as a separate collection of charts. I saw them as part of the main workflow. Users needed to see the current state of the information while working with it, rather than switch to a separate section and piece the data together themselves.

[[solution-2-fig]]

So I proposed showing analytical information directly in the context of the task. I tested the approach with users through an interactive prototype. Feedback was positive, and the subsequent changes concerned only which data to display and how to arrange it.

> **What I learned**
>
> The usefulness of analytics depends not only on what they show but also on when users see them. Sometimes putting data in the right place matters more than creating a separate screen with detailed visualizations.

## One interaction model for different tasks {#solution-3 label="Solution 03" sub="03"}

The product needed to handle large amounts of interconnected information. Different sections contained different content, but many of the actions users performed were similar.

I proposed a shared interaction model so users wouldn't have to learn the interface again each time they moved between sections. When designing complex forms, I drew on interviews, the materials I'd reviewed, and the requirements.

[[solution-3-fig]]

In the initial designs, I grouped related information and structured it based on my understanding of the workflows. I then reviewed the designs with users to see whether that organization matched how they actually worked.

This helped us check the completeness, accuracy, and presentation of information before development began. The overall structure worked well, but users preferred some fields in different positions. Small layout changes addressed those needs without changing the underlying interaction model.

> **What I learned**
>
> A structure can seem logical based on interviews and requirements, but testing it with users may reveal a more convenient way to organize information. It's worth keeping the overall interaction principles while staying flexible about the placement of individual elements.

## Outcomes {#results .finale}

[[results-finale-stat]]

The redesigned information structure, simplified navigation, and clearer presentation of complex data reduced the estimated effort required to work with documentation by a factor of three.

The initial product concept wasn't approved because its expected economic benefit was too limited. I identified opportunities to expand the system, proposed analytics capabilities, and helped demonstrate their business value.

The revised concept was approved, and the project moved into development.

Users responded positively to the proposed interfaces. We established a consistent interaction model across the system's sections and integrated analytics into everyday workflows.

## Reflections {#learnings .finale}

What interested me most about this project was that the original request didn't provide a strong enough reason to build the product. I had to explore additional opportunities and work out how the system could deliver more value to the business.

The experience reinforced something important for me: a product designer's work doesn't always begin with designing an interface. Sometimes the first question is whether the product should be built in the form originally requested at all.
